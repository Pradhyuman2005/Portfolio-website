Overview

Airbnb Co-Traveler is a native feature concept that allows solo travelers to safely discover compatible housemates and split multi-bedroom stays directly within the platform. Executed through a 5-day Google Design Sprint, I led the project from strategy and risk mapping down to building a production-ready component system in Figma, leveraging design tokens, auto-layout, and dynamic properties to deliver an end-to-end, high-converting checkout flow.

Problem

Travellers on Airbnb struggle to find compatible co-travellers for shared stays, often leading to mismatched expectations and uncomfortable experiences. There is also no streamlined way to discover and connect with like-minded individuals.

Discovery

Solo travel accounts for over 25% of Airbnb nights, yet guests lack a safe way to split multi-bedroom listings. Forced onto third-party forums, they face safety, payment, and compatibility risks.

A native co-traveler feature eliminates these barriers. To unlock high-tier inventory and boost conversions, I executed a 5-day Design Sprint to prototype a native co-traveler ecosystem built directly into Airbnb's existing design framework:-

  • Day 1 (Map & Align): Mapped primary business goals and customer journeys while stress-testing critical failure risks.
  • Day 2 (Sketch & Ideate): Designed matching logic, compatibility indicators, and split-checkout architecture.
  • Day 3 (Decide & Storyboard): Filtered high-impact solutions to build a frictionless, native user journey.
  • Day 4 (High-Fidelity Prototype): Built production-ready screens utilizing Airbnb's exact tokens, auto-layout patterns, and UI components.
  • Day 5 (User Test & Validate): Tested with solo travelers to confirm safety perception, flow clarity, and checkout intent.

Proposed Solution

A Native Co-Traveler Ecosystem integrated directly into Airbnb’s existing listing and checkout flows. By combining ID-verified profile gating, AI-assisted compatibility matching, and an escrowed split-payment model, this feature removes core safety and financial risks.

What we achieved

  • Native trust architecture eliminates social friction: By integrating verified profile gating, compatibility percentages, escrowed split payments natively into Airbnb's checkout, and half payment while booking to prevent last minute cancellations, we transformed a risky off-platform workaround into a secure, end-to-end guest experience.
  • Production-ready UI design system ensures immediate scalability: Leveraging reusable components, design tokens, auto-layout constraints, and dynamic component properties allowed us to expand Airbnb's existing design framework seamlessly without adding engineering debt.
  • Frictionless split-booking flow drives checkout conversion: Introducing clear preference matching and transparent cost allocation directly on multi-bedroom listings turns abandoned solo searches into high-value completed bookings.

Day 5: Trade-offs Made After Testing 5 Users

A few things I realized after designing high-fidelity & prototyping

  1. Changed the filter options
    Reason: I realised that the UI looked too cluncky and packed with testimonial and so many filter options. In the end I felt testimonial section wasn't needed because established users already trust Airbnb services

  1. Moved co-traveler discovery into a dedicated step following property selection
    Reason: Consolidating property evaluation, reviews, and housemate selection onto a single page created excessive cognitive load and overwhelmed users. Separating these actions streamlined decision-making and kept the booking flow intuitive.
  2. Replaced Heart Icon with a Bookmark
    Reason: User might get confused between saving and rating. Since the screen already used star icons for reviews, users often interpreted the heart as a "like" action rather than a save. A bookmark icon clearly communicates saving a property for later.

What’s Next?

Provide a visual layout tool where co-travelers can pre-assign bedrooms or amenities (e.g., primary bedroom vs. guest room) with adjusted cost-split ratios based on room tier.

Lessons Learned

  • Pragmatic design requires trimming the noise for core utility: I realized that packing the UI with testimonials alongside extensive filter options created a clunky, overwhelming interface. Recognizing that established users already trust Airbnb allowed me to remove redundant social proof and prioritize a cleaner, higher-converting search experience.
  • Familiar mental models always beat visual trends: Swapping the heart icon for a bookmark taught me to design around universal conventions rather than defaults. When star ratings are already present for reviews, users easily confuse hearts with "liking" rather than saving; using a bookmark eliminated ambiguity instantly.
  • Design with flexibility for inevitable tradeoffs: Prototyping on real viewports quickly reveals what works versus what clutters the screen. I learned to remain unattached to specific UI elements—like dense filter lists or extra content blocks—and adapt pragmatically to keep user cognitive load low.
